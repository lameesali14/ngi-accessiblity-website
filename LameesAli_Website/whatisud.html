What is UD?

Universal Design, or UD, is the process of making a setting so that everyone can access, understand, and use it to the fullest, no matter their age, size, ability, or condition. All people who want to use an area should be able to find what they need in any building, product, or service that is part of that area.

This is not a special condition for the benefit of a small group of people — it is one of the most important parts of good design. Everyone gains when a place is easy to get to, useful, convenient, and fun to use.

Universal design makes digital and physical spaces, services, and systems that work for everyone by taking their different skills and wants into account throughout the whole process of design. To put it simply, universal design is good design. (Centre for Excellence in Universal Design, 2024)

History of UD

In the 20th century, there were big changes in civil and human rights that led to longer life expectancy and more rights for disabled and older people. In response, governments passed laws against discrimination and for equal rights.

In the US, planner Ron Mace produced the term "universal design" and used it to focus on making homes more accessible. In the UK, Selwyn Goldsmith developed the idea of dropped kerbs, improving safety and convenience for both pedestrians and wheelchair users.

A key part of universal design is involving people from the early idea stage through to final development. In 2001, the Council of Europe Committee of Ministers introduced the idea of Universal Design. In 2009, the European Committee of Ministers advised member states to use Universal Design to promote full participation.

Since the European Conference on Design and Disability in Dublin in 1989, the Institute for Design and Disability (IDD) has been active. It was founded in 1991. With support from the EU Commission’s HORIZON program, the European Institute for Design and Disability (EIDD) was established in 1993. EIDD promotes the use of design to tackle social and environmental issues through research, consultancy, awareness campaigns, and knowledge sharing.

After the Disability Act was passed in 2005, the Centre for Excellence in Universal Design (CEUD) launched in January 2007. Industry responses to accessibility include barrier-free design, inclusive design, assistive technology, human-centred design, and co-design/participatory design.

These methods consider users' physical and behavioural needs right from the start of the design process, helping create solutions that fit people. Over time, Universal Design has evolved to include more areas and strategies to ensure accessibility and usability for everyone. (Centre for Excellence in Universal Design, n.d.)
